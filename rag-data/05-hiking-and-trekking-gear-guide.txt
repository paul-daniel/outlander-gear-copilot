Part 1: How to Choose a Hiking Backpack

The right backpack distributes weight efficiently, keeps your gear organized, and remains comfortable for hours on the trail. Choosing poorly means sore shoulders, an aching back, and a miserable experience.

Volume (Liters):
Backpacks are sized by their carrying capacity in liters. Here is a general guide:
- 10–20L: Day hikes and trail running. Enough for water, snacks, a rain jacket, and essentials. Our Trail Running 12L Vest falls in this range.
- 30–45L: Overnight to 2-night trips, or ultralight multi-day treks where you carry minimal gear.
- 50–70L: Multi-day treks (3–7 days) with full camping gear. Our 55L Hiking Backpack is in this sweet spot.
- 70L+: Extended expeditions, winter camping (bulkier gear), or thru-hikes with resupply points far apart.

Frame Type:
- Internal Frame: The standard for modern hiking packs. An internal aluminum (or composite) frame sheet and stays transfer weight to the hip belt. Our 55L pack uses an internal aluminum frame for excellent weight transfer on heavy loads.
- External Frame: Older design with a visible frame outside the bag. Rarely used today except for very heavy or awkwardly shaped loads.
- Frameless: Ultra-lightweight packs for fast-and-light hiking. Less comfortable with heavy loads but shave significant weight. Typically paired with a foam sleeping pad used as a back panel.

Suspension System:
A good suspension system is what makes the difference between a comfortable and an unbearable pack:
- Hip Belt: Should be well-padded and transfer 60–80% of the pack's weight to your hips. The hip belt on our 55L pack is generously padded with contoured foam.
- Shoulder Straps: Should be padded but not overly bulky. S-shaped straps are designed for women's bodies; J-shaped for men's.
- Load Lifter Straps: Connect the top of the shoulder straps to the top of the pack. When properly adjusted, they angle the pack's weight toward your center of gravity.
- Sternum Strap: A chest strap that prevents the shoulder straps from sliding outward. Should sit about 1 inch below your collarbone.
- Back Panel: Ventilated back panels (mesh or channeled foam) reduce sweat buildup. Critical for comfort in warm weather.

Features to Look For:
- Rain Cover: An integrated rain cover (like on our 55L pack) keeps your gear dry without the need to carry a separate cover. It stores in a small pocket at the base of the pack.
- Hydration Compatibility: A sleeve inside the main compartment for a water bladder, with routing holes for the drinking tube.
- Access Points: Top-loading is standard, but a U-shaped front zip (like on our 55L pack) allows you to access gear in the middle of the pack without unpacking everything from the top.
- Hip Belt Pockets: Convenient for snacks, phone, sunscreen, and other items you need without stopping.
- Compression Straps: Side straps that cinch the load closer to your back when the pack is not fully loaded, improving stability.
- Attachment Points: Daisy chains, ice axe loops, and trekking pole holders on the exterior.

Fitting a Backpack:
The most important measurement is your torso length (not your height). Measure from the C7 vertebra (the bony bump at the base of your neck) to the top of your hip bones. Packs come in different torso ranges (S, M, L) and the hip belt should be sized to your waist/hip measurement.

When trying on a pack: load it with 10–15 kg, tighten the hip belt first (it should sit on your iliac crest — the top of your hip bones), then adjust the shoulder straps, load lifters, and sternum strap.

Part 2: Trail Running Packs and Vests

Trail running demands ultra-lightweight, body-hugging packs that stay stable at speed. Our Trail Running 12L Vest is purpose-built for this:

Design Philosophy:
Trail running vests wrap around the front of your body like a vest (hence the name), distributing weight evenly across chest and back rather than pulling backward like a traditional pack. This keeps your center of gravity stable while running.

Key Features of Our 12L Trail Vest:
- 12-liter capacity: Enough for an ultra or a long day on the trail
- 1.5L hydration bladder included in a back panel sleeve
- Elastic chest pockets designed for soft flasks (500ml each), allowing you to drink without stopping
- Mesh back panel for ventilation during high-output activities
- Ultra-lightweight at just 320g (empty, without bladder)
- Bungee cords and compression straps to secure trekking poles

When to Choose a Vest vs. a Backpack:
Choose a vest for trail running, fast hiking, and any activity where you are moving quickly and want minimal bounce. Choose a traditional backpack when you need more capacity, better organization, or more comfortable carry for heavier loads over longer distances.

Part 3: How to Choose Trekking Poles

Trekking poles reduce impact on your knees by 25–30% on descents, improve balance on uneven terrain, and engage your upper body for a more efficient hiking gait. They are one of the most underrated pieces of hiking equipment.

Materials:
- Carbon Fiber: The lightest and most vibration-dampening material. Our Carbon Ultra Trekking Poles weigh just 380g per pair — exceptionally light. Carbon is ideal for long-distance hiking and trail running where every gram matters. The downside is that carbon can snap under extreme lateral stress (stepping on a pole), whereas aluminum bends but does not break.
- Aluminum: Heavier but nearly indestructible. Aluminum poles bend under extreme stress rather than snapping, making them field-repairable (you can straighten a bent pole). Better for bushwhacking, mountaineering, and rough use.

Adjustment Mechanisms:
- FlickLock (Lever Lock): Our Carbon Ultra poles use the FlickLock system — external levers that clamp the pole sections firmly. FlickLock is reliable, easy to adjust with gloves, and does not slip over time.
- Twist Lock: Internal expansion mechanism activated by twisting the pole sections. Lighter but can slip under heavy loads and is harder to operate with gloves or wet hands.
- Fixed Length / Folding: Some poles are not adjustable but fold into 3–4 sections connected by an internal cord (like an avalanche probe). Ultra-compact when packed but cannot be adjusted for different terrain.

Grip Material:
- Cork: Naturally wicks moisture, conforms to your hand shape over time, and provides excellent grip. Our Carbon Ultra poles feature cork grips. Cork is the premium choice for hiking.
- Foam (EVA): Soft, absorbent, and comfortable. Good alternative to cork, often found on trail running and ultralight poles.
- Rubber: Provides insulation in cold weather but does not wick moisture. Can cause blisters on long hikes in warm conditions.

Tip Material:
Carbide or tungsten tips provide the best grip on rock and hard terrain. Our poles feature tungsten tips — the hardest and most durable option, providing positive purchase on granite slabs and rocky trails.

Adjustable Length:
Our Carbon Ultra poles adjust from 65 cm to 135 cm, accommodating a wide range of heights and terrain. Shorten poles for uphills (to keep arms at roughly 90 degrees), lengthen for downhills (to reach the ground ahead).

Baskets:
Most poles come with small trekking baskets for dirt and rock trails. For snow, swap to large powder baskets (sold separately) to prevent the poles from plunging deep into soft snow.

Part 4: Clothing Layering for Hiking

While Outlander Gear Co. carries technical clothing (covered in our separate Clothing and Materials guides), here is a quick layering primer for hikers:

The 3-Layer System:
1. Base Layer: Moisture-wicking fabric (merino wool or synthetic) worn against your skin. Pulls sweat away from your body to keep you dry and regulate temperature.
2. Mid Layer: Insulating layer that traps body heat. A fleece or down jacket (like our Doudoune 700 Cuin) serves as the mid layer. Down is warmer and more compressible; fleece is easier to care for and works when wet.
3. Outer Layer (Shell): Wind and waterproof barrier. Our StormShield Waterproof Jacket (3-layer hardshell, 20,000 g/m²/24h breathability) is the ideal outer layer. It shields you from rain, snow, and wind while allowing perspiration to escape.

The beauty of layering is flexibility — you can add or remove layers as conditions change. Start a cold morning with all three layers, strip down to the base layer during a strenuous climb, and add the shell if rain moves in.
