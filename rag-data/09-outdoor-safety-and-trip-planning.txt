Part 1: The Ten Essentials

Originally compiled by The Mountaineers in the 1930s and updated over the decades, the Ten Essentials are items every outdoor adventurer should carry — regardless of how short or easy the trip seems.

1. Navigation:
Always carry a topographic map and compass — even if you also use GPS. Electronics fail: batteries die, screens crack, signals drop. Know how to use your compass with a map: orient the map to north, take bearings to landmarks, triangulate your position, and navigate to waypoints. These are skills that require practice before you need them.

Sunglasses (100% UV blocking), sunscreen (SPF 30+), and protective clothing. At altitude, UV exposure increases approximately 10–12% for every 1,000 meters of elevation gain. 5. First Aid Kit:
A basic kit should include: adhesive bandages, gauze, athletic tape, antiseptic wipes, pain relievers (ibuprofen), anti-inflammatory medication, blister treatments (moleskin), tweezers, safety pins, and any personal medications. Beyond your tent or planned shelter, carry an emergency bivy sack or space blanket. These weigh under 100g and can save your life if you are forced to spend an unplanned night out. They trap body heat and block wind.

Part 2: Weather Awareness and Planning

Reading the Weather:
Check the forecast before every trip, but understand that mountain weather is inherently unpredictable. Key warning signs in the field:
- Rapidly dropping barometric pressure (if you carry an altimeter watch)
- Building cumulus clouds, especially those with anvil-shaped tops (cumulonimbus — thunderstorms)
- Sudden temperature drops
- Increasing wind speed
- Fog or cloud descending to ground level

Thunderstorm Safety:
Lightning is one of the most dangerous hazards in the mountains. If a thunderstorm approaches:
- Descend from ridgelines, peaks, and exposed areas immediately.
- Avoid isolated tall trees, metal objects, and water.
- If caught in the open, move to a low area and crouch with feet together, minimizing contact with the ground.
- In a tent, avoid touching the poles (aluminum conducts electricity).
- Count the seconds between lightning flash and thunder — divide by 3 for approximate distance in kilometers. If under 10 seconds (3 km), you are in the danger zone.

Hypothermia:
Hypothermia occurs when your core body temperature drops below 35°C. It can happen even in summer if you are wet, tired, and in wind. Early symptoms include shivering, confusion, and loss of fine motor skills.

Prevention:
- Stay dry. Our StormShield jacket (waterproof, breathable) is your first line of defense. Layer with the Doudoune insulation layer.
- Eat and drink regularly — your body burns calories to generate heat.
- Monitor your group — hypothermia often progresses unnoticed by the victim.
- Carry extra dry layers in a waterproof bag.

Heat Exhaustion and Heatstroke:
In summer and at lower altitudes, heat is a serious risk. Symptoms include excessive sweating, nausea, dizziness, headache, and eventually cessation of sweating (a medical emergency).

Prevention:
- Drink before you are thirsty. Use our 1L insulated bottle (cold water for hours) and refill with the MiniPure filter.
- Wear light, breathable, UV-protective clothing (like our Convertible Trek Pants with UPF 50+).
- Take breaks in shade during the hottest hours (11 AM – 3 PM).
- Cover your head and neck.

Part 3: Trail Safety and Etiquette

Leave No Trace Principles:
At Outlander Gear Co., we are committed to preserving the outdoor spaces we enjoy. The seven Leave No Trace principles are:
1. Plan Ahead and Prepare — Know the regulations, weather, and terrain before you go.
2. Travel on Durable Surfaces — Stick to established trails and campsites. Walk on rock, gravel, or dry grass when off-trail.
3. Dispose of Waste Properly — Pack out ALL waste, including food scraps and toilet paper. Use a WAG bag or cat hole (15–20 cm deep, 60+ meters from water) for human waste.
4. Leave What You Find — Do not pick flowers, move rocks, or take "souvenirs" from nature.
5. Minimize Campfire Impact — Use a stove (like our GasPro) instead of a campfire when possible. If fires are allowed, use established fire rings and keep fires small.
6. Respect Wildlife — Observe from a distance. Do not feed animals. Store food in bear canisters or hang from a bear line where required.
7. Be Considerate of Other Visitors — Yield the trail to uphill hikers. Keep noise levels low. Camp out of sight and sound of trails and other campers.

Trail Right-of-Way:
- Uphill hikers have the right of way (they have a harder time stopping and restarting).
- Hikers yield to horses.
- Mountain bikers yield to both hikers and horses.
- Step to the downhill side of the trail when yielding.

Part 4: Trip Planning Checklist

Before any outdoor trip, work through this checklist:

Route Planning:
☐ Study the map and know the route, including distances, elevation gain, water sources, and escape routes.
☐ Check trail conditions and closures (local ranger stations, recent trip reports).
☐ Estimate time using Naismith's Rule: 5 km per hour on flat terrain, plus 30 minutes per 300 meters of elevation gain. Add time for breaks, photos, and going slower than planned.
☐ Identify bail-out points — places where you can cut the trip short and return safely if conditions deteriorate or someone is injured.

Gear Check:
☐ Ten Essentials packed (see list above).
☐ All gear inspected — tent seams, sleeping bag loft, stove function, headlamp batteries, water filter integrity.
☐ Clothing appropriate for the worst forecast weather, not just the best.
☐ Footwear broken in and appropriate for the terrain (see our Footwear Guide).
☐ Backpack fits properly and is not overpacked. Target 20% of body weight max for hiking.

Communication:
☐ Leave a detailed trip plan with a trusted person: where you are going, your planned route, expected return time, and instructions for when to call for help.
☐ Carry a charged phone in a waterproof case. Note: cell coverage may be limited or nonexistent in remote areas.
☐ Consider a satellite communicator (e.g., Garmin inReach) for trips beyond cell coverage — these allow you to send SOS signals and text messages via satellite.

Physical Preparation:
☐ Be honest about your fitness level. Train for the demands of the trip (elevation gain, pack weight, distance, altitude).
☐ Acclimatize gradually if going to altitude above 2,500 meters.
☐ Know your group members' abilities and plan for the weakest member.

Part 5: Emergency Procedures

If Someone is Injured:
1. Ensure the scene is safe (no ongoing rockfall, avalanche risk, etc.).
2. Assess the injury using a systematic approach (head-to-toe survey).
3. Administer first aid with your kit.
4. Decide: Can the person walk out? Do you need to call for rescue?
5. Call emergency services if needed (112 in EU, 15 for SAMU in France, 18 for fire/rescue in France).
6. Provide your exact GPS coordinates, number of patients, nature of injuries, and weather conditions.
7. Stay with the injured person unless you must go for help and it is safer to do so.

If You Are Lost:
1. STOP: Sit down, Think, Observe, Plan.
2. Do not continue walking hoping to find the trail — this usually makes things worse.
3. Use your compass and map to determine your approximate location.
4. If you can safely retrace your steps to a known point, do so.
5. If not, stay put and signal for help (whistle — 3 blasts is the international distress signal — repeated every few minutes).
6. Make yourself visible — wear bright clothing, use a mirror (like the one on our sighting compass) to flash sunlight toward searchers.
